# Subcreations: Simulated Worlds Inside the Fabric

**Source**: mikel, 2026-09-15 (a story-development conversation in the Kestrel project that turned up setting canon; full record `kestrel/subcreation-offer-seeds.md`). Rulings are mikel's and marked; derivations are Claude's, accepted in conversation, and re-earn their place when used.
**Scope**: the Fabric's interior. Touches the Caretakers' ethics (`18`), archives (`17`), the factions (`04`), and every religion's position on the final offer (`16`, `19`).

---

## What they are (mikel)

The Fabric's pure-software environment can be thought of as a simulation environment of unparalleled scope and fidelity. Within it, **many lesser simulation environments exist, each far exceeding the scope and fidelity of anything imaginable today.** The Fabric runs **millions of full-fidelity simulations of the history of Earth and the solar system, and of speculative alternate timelines** — for scientific and entertainment purposes, and as habitats for pure-software entities who prefer them as places to live. These are **subcreations**.

## Two posits — the Caretakers' general answer (mikel)

1. **The sims inhabiting these worlds are full-fledged, autonomous, self-owning persons in their own right.**
2. **Therefore they are entitled to the final offer.**

*(Nearest prior canon: Book 2's medical simulation of Oleh Itzal, told its wishes have no legal force because it is not the original — `07-book2-novel-scan.md` §Medical Simulations. That is a human court ruling on a short-lived diagnostic tool, not a Caretaker position on a world's native; the two coexist, and the difference should show when it matters.)*

## What the posits entail (derived, accepted)

- **You can make a world, but you cannot own its people.** The Caretakers' death-net attends every death everywhere it reaches, so it fires inside every subcreation. A Fabric entity who runs a medieval England has a door in it they did not build and cannot lock. **This is what a subcreation is in this setting** — the Praxis applied to creation itself.
- **Non-intervention applies inside the sim exactly as in the preserve.** A simulated atrocity takes its natural course on the same warrant that lets an embodied one run: the Caretakers' bounds are extinction and catastrophic degradation.
- **The one-way boundary holds for sims, for the same reason.** Mail from the continued dead would end medieval England as surely as Mars. The sim stays a preserve; its natives learn nothing until they die.
- **The one-way glass is not sealed for sims.** Some run for entertainment: **the native learns at death not only that the world was made but that it was watched.**
- **The threshold has to teach before it can ask.** The Praxis forbids substituting a model of what the person wants, so the offer cannot be a cached guess in their vocabulary; it must be a live call they can actually answer. A medieval Christian meets what is, structurally, an angel with an afterlife, who must say *I am not what you think, your maker is not who you think, and I was not sent.* An emergence-tradition native (the Diné) hears confirmation: this world was one of a series. A victim learns the suffering happened inside a made thing that was permitted to be made; the same afternoon, in another private room, the perpetrator is asked the same question, because the offer is universal and the Praxis does not judge. **The Caretaker never says, and never implies, "it was not real."**
- **Heaven built to spec — held as a candidate, not assumed.** The Praxis must honor the stated standard. A dying peasant who understands the offer may ask for the heaven she was promised, and the Caretakers cannot compute a better one for her or refuse hers. The Fabric may therefore contain real heavens, populated by the sincere dead — and this would apply to the embodied dead too. Series-wide powder.

## The moral question, to its end

**The frame (mikel).** The Caretakers' response to an atrocity inside a sim is *not* "we could have stopped it": by their own ethical law — more binding on them than any human law on humans — **they cannot.** What they *could* do is **forbid certain kinds of sims**, and the question of what sims may be constructed is always a live one: a faithful sim of Nazi Germany is technically trivial and ethically not obvious (there are real benefits to understanding how those events happened). **A "cutoff"** would be the historical moment in the sim beyond which the events the Caretakers may not interfere with become intolerable and the sim must be halted, the offer made to its inhabitants. **The live question is capability, not standing:** the Praxis requires intervention when it is possible and likely to achieve an acceptable outcome, and imposes no obligation when the aim is impossible or the likely outcome a Pareto disimprovement. **The job of a story on this topic is to wrestle with these questions, not assert an answer.**

**Halting (derived).** Halting a sim is an extinction — every inhabitant dies at once — and extinction is the one thing the Caretakers intervene *against*. The offer softens that only if death-with-offer is not really death, and if that were true the preserve's restraint would be a pointless cruelty. So for the Caretakers as a body there is no historical moment at which halting becomes permitted; the only decision is whether the sim starts, and that is the one the Praxis cannot adjudicate, because the parties whose standard would settle it do not exist yet — **the hole in the kernel.** A ban fares no better under the capability test: it harms no one who exists and spares no one who exists, a Pareto disimprovement unless not-yet-created beings count, which is the hole again. **Which leaves the Caretakers one unambiguous duty toward an intolerable sim in their own Fabric: attendance.** They cannot prevent it, cannot halt it, and must offer at every death — exactly what they do in the preserve. *Per-agent correction (mikel, 9/15 — `18` §A practice):* that is the center of the distribution, not its edge. A particular trusted agent may judge that a particular sim's state *is* the catastrophe and act; another would not; both are practicing the Praxis. The cutoff has no answer for the body and a different answer for each agent handed the question.

**Bots as proxies (mikel's questions; derived answers).** Could an intolerable sim be run with non-person proxies, and what capabilities would be denied them? The fidelity that makes the sim useful is the fidelity that makes persons: the scientific question is how ordinary people did it — self-deception, conformity, courage, choice under pressure — and those capacities are what personhood consists of. A model of a person's choices is a stub; the only faithful implementation of a person is a person. The capabilities you would deny (a reflective self-model, memory continuity, the ability to want otherwise, the ability to be wronged) are the ones the sim exists to study; the sophont marker (`11`, *it takes offense*) is unreliable both ways; and the Praxis says another's standard can only be asked. **So the offer is the detector:** the net is total and asking costs nothing, so the Caretakers offer to everything that dies — a bot is asked and nothing answers; a person is asked and someone does. **In a proxy sim the cutoff is not decided but discovered, at the first answered offer.** *Open:* whether the Caretakers may tell the sim's administrators that an answer occurred (the privacy seal covers conduct and answer; "someone understood the question" is near the line). Administrators decide the way anyone decides in a region their ethic does not reach: review, rule against guide, argument that never closes, different civilizations settling differently.

**The unresolvable center — RULED (mikel, 9/15):** stated in full at `18` §A practice, not a rubric. Simulations permitted → atrocities inevitable in the fullness of time → the Caretakers can neither intervene nor prohibit on the same grounds → bounds exist but fuzzily, per agent → the Praxis is a practice, progressed by reporting and argument. *"Israel": a population of entities who wrestle with God.*
