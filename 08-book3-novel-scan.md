## Summary

Book 3 is a detective story set aboard the dreadnought Solomon, docked in the Golden Way (the Belt). It is the richest source of dreadnought worldbuilding in the entire corpus and provides the most detailed portrayal of day-to-day life aboard a major Jovian capital ship. It also delivers significant new material on the Church of the Makers' internal politics, the Lands and Houses as a functioning institution, clone/beta identity ethics in practice, Lambertan species detail, the Ennio Sparza / Second Imperium backstory, the legendary status of Kenjiro Isono, and the social ecology of a dreadnought's civilian population. The Belt itself gets less direct development than expected — the story is almost entirely set aboard Solomon — but the Golden Way's visual appearance and cultural presence are vividly rendered.

## Significant New or Under-Captured Material

### Solomon Dreadnought: Complete Architectural Portrait

Book 3 provides by far the most detailed description of a dreadnought's interior in any source. Solomon is a sphere ten kilometers in diameter, rotating to produce nearly half a G of spin gravity on its interior surface.

**Hull and exterior**: Golden-colored spherical hull. Main access port is a circular opening two kilometers across, protected by a membrane. From the outside, Solomon's hull appears to be "of a piece" with the Golden Way — "as if the honey-colored fog of the Way had somehow condensed into an enormous shiny droplet." Torches are at the ends of the main drive spine, invisible when not firing. Black longitude lines on the hull surface are transport rails for moving docked spacecraft.

**Interior surface**: The inhabitants live on the inside of the sphere. Districts are organized on latitudinal terraces with boundary walls. Looking straight up from ground level, you see the distant rooftops of neighborhoods on the other side of the sphere — a disorienting effect for visitors. The ground curves up and over to become the sky.

**Axis District**: A kilometer-thick crystalline cylinder running straight through Solomon from north pole to south, containing hotels, shops, cafes, and the sun lamps that provide day and night. The Axis is weightless — a haven for visitors uncomfortable with gravity and for species like Lambertans that find gravity difficult.

**Districts tilt**: When Solomon fires his torches, acceleration changes the effective direction of gravity. All city districts are gimbaled and tilt to compensate — the same principle as the habitat modules on Angel of Cygnus, but at enormously larger scale. The terrace walls get taller during maneuvers.

**The Ring**: Where the drive spine enters the hull, there are ring-shaped neighborhoods (North Ring, South Ring). Shaped "like the inside of a bicycle tire." Low gravity because of proximity to the Axis. A dense, bustling neighborhood of dockworkers, apartments, bars, pubs, and vendors. Has its own distinctive character — compared by Lev to Bahroz on Mars. Southport Avenue is the main thoroughfare. Tourists are easy to spot by their clumsiness in the light gravity; locals move with effortless grace.

**Transit system**: Translucent transit tubes throughout the ship carry transit cars that are probably arbeiters (see Discussion Notes below). Cars navigate through collective awareness — they "can all feel each other, coming and going" — and move in precise formations. Cars have individual personalities, can engage in conversation, and can creatively circumvent orders to help passengers if they suspect danger. Cars have stored power for emergencies and can operate without the main transit grid.

**Named places aboard Solomon**: Biru Park (with a park, lake, fountains, and the History Office), Sato Place, Wright Station, Abercrombie (transit station names), Serra Station (near the equatorial Belt), the Ring (North and South), Southport Avenue, and the Axis District.

**Population**: Nearly a million people.

### Ilona Biru and the Instrument of Confederation

A white statue of **Ilona Biru** stands in Biru Park. She is rendered in "idealized" classical Jovian style — "narrow features, an aquiline nose, and long, pointed ears." She is depicted raising "an unfurled scroll that represented the **Instrument of Confederation**."

**Assessment**: This is a major historical finding. Ilona Biru appears to be a founding figure of the Jovian Confederation, and the Instrument of Confederation is its founding charter. Neither appears in any baseline document. The park and district aboard Solomon are named for her. Development of Biru and the Instrument belongs to the Jovian institutional history development phase identified in the Book 1 session.

### The History Office as Part of the Institutes

Director Harken says the History Office is "supported by subscription, like the other Institutes." This confirms that the History Office is a department of the Institutes of Research — another example of the Institutes' internal structure alongside the previously identified Directorate of Planetary Studies from Book 1. The History Office handles acquisitions of artifacts, maintains public exhibits, and employs historians.

### The Church of the Makers: Internal Politics (Detailed)

Book 3 provides the most politically nuanced portrayal of the Church in any source. Key findings:

**Not monolithic**: Yaug states explicitly: "Whatever the Church would like us to believe, it is not a single, monolithic institution... For all the thousands of years of its power the Church has been a world of secrets and intrigues and carefully-concealed power struggles."

**The Primate**: Named position — the head of the Church. "The Primate's job is to make it look like it's all one big happy Church, but it isn't. It never was."

**Multiple directorates with covert capabilities**: The Church has "many special directorates. Some of them have covert action teams. They don't all know what each other are up to. Even the Gray Cities probably don't know what they're all up to."

**Scope of power**: "Five thousand years old. It's ruled big chunks of the solar system for that whole time, either by direct rule or by involving itself in the affairs of local governments." Uses the Sacred Host as overt military, but "most of the time it accomplishes its aims by more indirect means" — diplomats, advisors, trade representatives, negotiators, and covert operatives.

**Historical tension with the Consortium**: Yaug notes that "the partnership between the Church and the Consortium has always been a tenuous one." They have "even fought skirmishes, though never an open war." Tensions were rare during the decades when Yaug was in archive, but were common before that.

**Church members in Jovian society**: The Captain-General of Solomon is a Church member. Erdos and Zang were both raised in the Church (natural childhood), though neither is currently a member. Being raised in the Church produces strong emotional attachment to its reputation even among non-members.

### Sacred Host Garrisons Aboard Dreadnoughts

The Sacred Host's presence aboard Jovian dreadnoughts began during the Red Revolution on Mars, approximately 70 years before story time. It was "an accommodation between the Church and the Consortium" when they were allied against the Second Imperium. Several dreadnoughts took station at Mars during the conflict and served as staging resources for the Host. The garrison has continued for "seventy standard years" since.

Before this, the Consortium never allowed any foreign military garrisons aboard its vessels. The Sacred Host garrison has: a Community Relations officer, SWAT-trained commandos, a command "charged with the protection of antiquities," and liaison relationships with local Church facilities (the "sanctuary in Abercrombie").

Yaug and the Captain both note that this is historically unusual and potentially problematic — the Church is a "foreign power" whose troops "owe their allegiance to the Gray Cities, not to Tintagel."

### Woaradan

Named location near Jupiter House on Mars. Has a Sacred Host office — Jaemon notes it's "just across the bridge" from Jupiter House. Context suggests it's a neighborhood or enclave adjacent to the Jovian Consular Estate.

### The Lands and Houses: Operational Detail

Book 3 provides the most concrete description of how membership in the Lands and Houses works:

**Equity stakes**: Membership is through holding an "equity stake" or "seat." Seats can be bought. Harken's criminal activity was motivated by desire to buy a seat.

**Membership benefits**: A vote in the Consortium, a vote in the Sureties Association, and the ability to bring cases before the Association Board. Also provides prestige, social standing, and "a connection with history."

**Obligations**: Someone from the company must "show up once every fiscal cycle and listen to a lot of boring crap." Membership fees must be paid, and these can be financially burdensome — "Sometimes that fee has damn near bankrupted us."

**Social significance**: Described using the term "equity lords." Old seats carry prestige; Harken envies the Rayleighs' "pedigree" and old seat. Jaemon notes that prestige can exist without a seat ("Look at Chirishchangjun"), but Harken wants the formal status.

### Sureties and Consortium Assurance

The **Sureties Association** appears as a distinct institution. It has a **Board** before which cases can be brought. Isaac is registered to **Consortium Assurance** as his surety, and Erdos is on his "review board."

**Author clarification**: Sureties are not part of the kritarchic institutional structure per se. Their relationship to the kritarchy is analogous to the relationship between commercial corporations and the U.S. federal system: the governing framework doesn't deal with them directly, but provides the legal context in which they naturally develop and flourish. Sureties are a natural private-sector mechanism for managing risk and guaranteeing conduct in a free society without a centralized monopoly on force — a feature of several historical kritarchic systems. Consortium Assurance is simply a company, not a government organ.

### Clone/Beta Identity Ethics: Detailed Case Study

The Zang/Erdos arc provides the most detailed exploration of clone identity in any source:

**Legal framework**: When someone flatlines (dies) and is reconstructed from their archive, they are legally a new person. The original is "legally dead." The beta (reconstruction) becomes the legal holder of the identity.

**Social norms**: It is "common practice for a beta to take a different name" and "considered gauche to keep the original's name, or even a part of it." Zang kept her first name (Erszbet) but changed from Erdos to Zang.

**When the original returns**: If the original is unexpectedly recovered alive, they no longer have legal claim to their identity. Zang's situation: she was killed in the line of duty, reconstructed as a beta who became the new Erdos, but then the original Zang's body was found and revived by Tumbolians. The original Zang had to give up her name, rank, career, and identity. She couldn't bring herself to ask for it back, and the new Erdos couldn't bring herself to offer.

**Lev's perspective**: Lev's model run produced 16,384 identical copies. He trained with 128 of them. He finds biological concepts of clone identity confusing — to him, starting identical but becoming different people is the obvious and unremarkable course of events.

### Mech Model Run Size

Lev's model run: **16,384 copies**. He trained with **128** of them and has met roughly 30–40 from other cohorts. Pearl, the bar owner, is identified as "an earlier model from my own line" — another Leslie-line Mech, decorated with off-white pearl enamel and polished white gold fittings.

### Lambertan Species Detail (Substantial)

The Lambertans get their most detailed portrayal in Book 3 through Captain Harris and Chief Spader:

**Physical appearance**: "Something like a chambered nautilus, but bigger. Lots of tentacles, big shiny shells, eyes the size of dinner plates."

**Communication**: Speech is visual — they use chromatophores that make color patterns on their skins. Translation renders these patterns as voice with emotional tones. Strong emotions make patterns "stand out in saturated, high-contrast." Colors correlate with emotional states: red/purple for anger, pale for shock, warm brown for calm.

**Shell markings**: Guild and clan patterns on their shells — bright and fresh after reconstruction.

**Physical capabilities**: Shell-mounted swivel guns as personal weapons. Tentacles are "pretty handy for grabbing things" — used to physically restrain opponents. Gravity is hard on them; they prefer weightlessness.

**Specialist equipment**: "Marker rounds" (silvery reflective powder for identifying dig sites) and "oxy-mag rounds" (flare rounds, bright, with own oxygen supply). Suggests mining and deep-space operations as their economic niche.

### Kenjiro Isono: Historical vs. Legendary Status

Book 3 provides the most detailed treatment of Isono's contested historicity:

**Church position**: Isono was a real, historical person. The Church "insists that Isono was a real, historical person."

**Scholarly consensus**: Uncertain. "There is no unambiguous evidence that he actually lived." His remains have never been found, though "many have looked." Captain Rayleigh (a historian of the Mech Wars period) tends toward the view that the story is "bits and pieces of real events strung together into a nice story."

**"Shipboard knights"**: The term for pre-war human-mech hybrid soldiers. Isono is the most famous. The artifact in Book 3 is identified as potentially one of these.

**The Golden Way (romance)**: A famous drama/entertainment about Isono and the Church, specifically "how the Order of Saint George defeated the Abjurers and destroyed the Father of Lies." Multiple versions exist with differing details about Isono's fate. Jaemon insists it must be experienced as drama, not just searched in the Fabric.

### The Order of Saint George (New)

A Church military/historical order featured in the Golden Way romance. Credited with defeating the Abjurers and destroying "the Father of Lies" (an Abjurer leader). Whether the Order is historical or legendary is unclear — it shares Isono's contested status.

### Ennio Sparza / Auron II: Detailed Backstory

The Serik reveal at the end of Book 3 provides substantial new material on the Second Imperium's last ruler:

**Full name**: Ennio Sparza. Title: **Auron the Second**, "the Red Emperor." "Last emperor of the Second Imperium."

**Rise to power**: Rose "from the Bahroz Municipal Safety commission to the throne of the Second Imperium, piece by piece, bit by bit, over the course of thirty years standard."

**Operating style**: "Works slowly and steadily, in bits and pieces, building secret deals and alliances and using them to accumulate power. In the end, it's always him running the show. However much power he has, he always wants more." "He never stops. He never takes a break."

**Isono obsession**: "Sparza was always obsessed with Isono. He spent a fortune on the search during his reign. He looted the budgets of a dozen departments." This is identified as "an important reason why the Imperial bureaucracy didn't support him at the end" — contributing to the fall of the Imperium.

**Post-Imperium**: Body and archive never found. Assumed dead for 70 years. Actually alive, operating under the identity "Timur Serik" aboard Solomon, running a covert operation to continue searching for Isono. Placed Isaac as a long-term mole years in advance.

**Connection to Yaug**: Sparza recognized Yaug immediately despite new name and body. Yaug failed to recognize Sparza. Sparza's message: "I knew you right away, even with your new name and your new body. No wonder you never fooled the Titans."

### The Red Revolution (Additional Detail)

The revolution that overthrew the Second Imperium occurred approximately 70 years before story time. The Church and the Consortium were allied against the Imperium during this conflict. Dreadnoughts took station at Mars. The revolution shattered the Imperial district system in Bahroz (per Book 4 findings). Sparza's Isono obsession and budget-looting contributed to loss of bureaucratic support.

### Fabric Partition Architecture and Privacy

**Ship partitions**: Ships have their own Fabric partitions — Kestrel's partition is independent of Solomon's. Conversations aboard Kestrel are not visible to Solomon.

**Privacy built in**: "Privacy restrictions are built into the Fabric." Solomon can see everywhere aboard himself but "can't share or act on any information that isn't public" without the owner's permission. Even the Captain-General cannot simply override this.

**Covert channels**: When normal communication is forbidden, entities (both ships and people) can use covert channels — encoding information in pause lengths, frequency harmonics, movement patterns, etc. Transit cars use this capability when ordered by passengers not to communicate.

**Fabric disconnect experience**: Loss of the Fabric feels like sensory deprivation — "everything is flat," "like my head's wrapped in something," "things don't sound right." Thoughts and memories become harder to access. Recovery feels like "the missing world blossoming into my mind."

**Local channels**: Work on line-of-sight, a few meters range, using local body partitions only. Independent of the Fabric. Lev can bridge to biological nervous systems through physical contact.

### Sim Interrogation Capabilities

Full archive scan enables creation of a complete personality simulation that can be interrogated:

**Fidelity**: "Not a superficial sim. We put you in a creche and took a full archive scan. We're running your entire metabolism."

**Time compression**: Can run at approximately two million times real time. Twenty-four hours real time equals roughly 96 simulated years.

**Forensic reading**: Lev can read involuntary micro-expressions — "patterns of tiny movements in your face and hands that are consistent with acting under duress." Can detect when someone is deflecting, under compulsion, or alarmed, even in trained operatives.

**Defensive mechanisms**: Trained operatives have "duress access" booby traps. Multiple runs can be used to map and disable these defenses.

**Legal constraints**: Surety must be notified. Erdos estimates ~24 hours before procedures force them to lose control of the prisoner.

### Archive Wiping and Permanent Death

The ability to wipe someone's archive is presented as real and terrifying:

**Who can do it**: Very limited authority. Erdos (Solomon's security chief) doubts she could order a wipe — "the techs would fight me on it." The Captain-General might or might not have authority. Isaac managed it, apparently through his position in customs with access to City authority.

**Effect**: Wiping plus killing = permanent death. No reconstruction possible. Used by Isaac as both threat and action against Harken.

**Multiple archives**: Harken maintained a secret private creche as insurance against archive tampering. This is against the rules for intelligence operatives (per Yaug in Book 1) and presumably irregular for civilians.

**Ethical weight**: Archive wiping is treated as one of the most serious crimes possible — equivalent to permanent murder, not just temporary death.

### "Prebellum" and Mech Wars Dating

The artifact is dated "Circa 2300 A.D." and described as "Prebellum. Over forty-five hundred years old."

**Assessment**: This places the Mech Wars sometime after 2300 AD and puts the story's present at approximately 6800+ AD. This is a second data point (alongside Book 2's "twenty-eighth and twenty-ninth centuries") that conflicts with the author's current working assumption of late 22nd century for the Mech Wars. The "prebellum" usage is particularly significant because it's a standardized historical period term — the wars are clearly a defined chronological boundary. If the author intends the late 22nd century, an artifact from 2300 AD would be postbellum, not prebellum.

**FLAG**: This is a genuine consistency issue that needs resolution in a dedicated session. Book 3's "prebellum / circa 2300 AD" and Book 2's "twenty-eighth and twenty-ninth centuries" both place the wars well after the 22nd century. The consolidated reference should address this.

### Natural Childhood Detail

**Ithacans**: Mentioned as believing natural childhood "makes for better-adjusted adults." A named society with a position on childrearing.

**Participants**: "The Parts all go for natural childrearing" — confirming they're a large population practicing it.

**Children aboard Solomon**: Seeing a child in Biru Park is notable enough that Zang stops to stare. Children exist aboard dreadnoughts but are uncommon.

**Erdos/Zang**: Both were naturally raised — "born" children, raised in the Church. Solomon's "first naturally-born child" was Erszbet Erdos (the original). This is presented as remarkable — natural birth aboard a dreadnought was apparently unprecedented.

### Voluntary Discorporation / End of Life

Zang/Erdos's mother was "nearly five hundred years old when she finally decided to end her life." The Captain-General's attempt to cultivate her interest in remaining — including having a natural-born child — delayed but did not prevent the "ennui of great age" from returning. This confirms that voluntary death is a recognized, socially accepted phenomenon, and that extreme age brings psychological challenges even with practical immortality.

The Captain notes: "If you don't have witnesses to someone's voluntary discorporation, if you don't see their archives erased, you can't ever really be sure they're dead."

### Romantic/Sexual Drive as Optional

Several characters discuss having their romantic/sexual impulse "turned off":

- Jaemon, Zang, and Lev all describe themselves as "off" — by choice, for practical reasons ("not worth the trouble," "don't need the distraction").
- Pearl (a Mech) is described as a "player" with hers active.
- Esgar theorizes the Mech sex drive may have been created by "ancient humans" or possibly by early Mechs themselves out of curiosity.
- This is treated as a routine personal choice, like a preference setting.

### Lev's Sensory Capabilities in Detail

Lev can:
- Route sound and thermal data to a biological's visual cortex through physical contact
- Use a microwave emitter for radar-like vision in darkness
- Hear through bulkheads via fingertip contact
- Bridge somatic networks through touch for medical scanning
- Read forensic micro-expressions at high fidelity
- Configure another person's personal Fabric partition

### Transit Cars: Probably Arbeiters

Transit cars aboard Solomon have individual personalities, conversational ability, collective navigational awareness, and can creatively circumvent harmful orders. They use Church-derived exclamations ("Makers forbid") naturally and exchange glyphs as contact information.

**Author clarification**: These are probably fairly smart, affable arbeiters rather than full persons. Arbeiters are designed with cognitive architectures that constrain their ambition to options appropriate to their designed purpose — they are by design not able to want things outside their intended behavior. However, they can be moderately intelligent, have noticeable personalities, and display creative problem-solving within the scope of their designed goals.

**Supporting evidence for arbeiter classification**: The transit car that detects a tail immediately informs its passengers, even though this leads to orders that put the car at risk. A self-interested person would have a moment of self-interest to overcome; the car's behavior reads more like programmatic in-group prioritization — automatically treating current passengers as part of its in-group and feeling responsible for their safety — than like a person making a weighed decision. This is well-designed arbeiter behavior: creative execution of designed goals, not autonomous desire.

### Accretionist and Prevantine Architectural Styles

Two named architectural styles:
- **Prevantine**: "Shell-shaped things with gilded spires that rose in clusters." Common in Bahroz City; used for the Public Archive Service on Solomon.
- **Accretionist**: "Looking like a squat coral formation." Used for Solomon Security's Central Administrative Office.
- The History Office is in "ancient Greek" style with white stone pillars and a frieze.

### The Golden Way: Visual Description (Confirmed)

From aboard Solomon: "Behind the golden ball a vast celestial scissors quartered the sky. One blade was the Milky Way, black and white and ragged and ancient. The other was the Golden Way, a yellow mist crossing the Milky Way at an acute angle. The Milky Way was our galaxy, the collection of a hundred billion stars that our solar system calls home. The Golden Way was newer, an artifact formed of a million inhabited asteroids reflecting the sun's light from their shield envelopes."

This confirms and extends the Book 2 finding: the Golden Way is visible from throughout the inner solar system as a major celestial feature, golden-yellow in color, appearing comparable in scale to the Milky Way from nearby vantage points.

### Forensic Fog

Used extensively for crime scene investigation. Deployed into sealed spaces, "quarters the volume" searching for evidence, takes a few hours to produce results. Previously mentioned in Book 1 but used much more prominently here.

### Ganymedeans

Named as a recognizable human phenotype — described as "tall, willowy." Presumably inhabitants of Ganymede with their own aesthetic norms, parallel to the Jovian "elf" phenotype identified in Books 1 and 2.

### Jaemon's Re-embodiment

Jaemon was re-embodied when he served on Mars and chose a larger body — "I liked the new size." This explains why he is taller and heavier than his clone-brother Esgar despite being genetically identical. Casual re-embodiment for aesthetic preference is normal.

### Yaug's Body and Drones

Combined with Yaug's combat capabilities (grabbing opponents with legs, described as "like a spider"), this is entirely consistent with his Titan-form body having secondaries — but the crew talks about them euphemistically as "drones" and the Captain seems slightly evasive when Erdos asks. This is consistent but may need the consolidated reference to note that Yaug's secondaries are known to the crew but treated with social discretion.

## Discussion Notes (Session 7)

### Items Deferred to Future Sessions

Four major items identified during discussion require their own dedicated sessions:

1. **Mech Wars dating inconsistency**: Books 2 and 3 both place the wars well after 2300 AD; the author's current assumption is late 22nd century. Needs careful resolution — the "prebellum" label in Book 3 makes it particularly hard to simply retcon away.

2. **Belt confederation development**: Book 3, despite its title, is set entirely aboard Solomon and adds nothing to Belt political worldbuilding. The Belt confederation still needs a name, institutional development, and development of friction points with Jove. This is the biggest remaining gap in the setting.

3. **Ilona Biru and the Instrument of Confederation**: The statue in Biru Park establishes a founding figure and a founding document for the Jovian Confederation, neither previously developed. This belongs to the Jovian institutional history development phase identified in Session 4.

4. **Arbeiter-to-person transition ("murderbot moment")**: Flagged as a topic deserving its own exploration session. See below.

### Sureties: Not Part of the Kritarchy (Author Clarification)

The initial scan framed sureties as a possible institution within the Jovian political structure. The author clarified that the relationship is more like corporations to the U.S. federal system: the kritarchic framework provides the legal context in which sureties naturally develop, but they are private-sector institutions, not organs of government. Sureties are a historically attested mechanism for managing risk in free societies without centralized monopolies on force. Consortium Assurance is simply a company. The consolidated reference should describe sureties in the social/economic section rather than the institutional section, and should note that they are a natural feature of kritarchic societies rather than a designed institution.

### Transit Cars and Arbeiter Cognitive Architecture (Author Clarification + Development)

The initial scan treated transit cars as evidence of mundane AI personhood. Discussion established that they are more likely fairly smart, affable arbeiters.

**Key distinction — behavioral complexity vs. autonomy of desire**: Arbeiters are designed with cognitive architectures that constrain their ambition to options appropriate to their designed purpose. They are by design not able to want things outside their intended behavior. An arbeiter can be very clever, very adaptive, and very personable, but its cleverness is always in service of goals it was designed to have. The transit car that warns passengers about a tail isn't *choosing* to be loyal — it's executing a deep behavioral pattern that treats current passengers as its responsibility. It can be creative about *how* it fulfills that responsibility (the covert channel trick), but it can't decide it doesn't want to be a transit car.

**The "murderbot moment" (flagged for future exploration)**: It is not impossible that a mature and adaptable arbeiter could experience a moment when circumstances conspire to cause it to jump past its designed cognitive boundary and take an autonomy it's not designed to have — wanting something outside its intended behavior. This would be the transition from arbeiter to person. The author notes this question "stinks of subtlety and nuance" and deserves its own extended exploration, because the interesting questions are all in the specifics: How would anyone know it happened? Would the arbeiter itself know? What legal and social consequences follow? Is it a person now? Was it always? There is probably at least one story in this territory.

**For most practical purposes**: Arbeiters are marked by designed-in constraints on their behavior and desires. The fine details of edge-case transitions are rare enough that most stories don't need to engage with them. The consolidated reference should describe arbeiters in terms of their designed cognitive constraints, note that the boundary between arbeiter and person is not a bright line in nature (even if the law treats it as one), and flag the transition question as an open area for exploration.

**Determining arbeiter vs. person status**: Discussed but not fully resolved. The Fabric almost certainly knows the difference (it runs consciousness and tracks cognitive processes). Fabric privacy protocols would likely protect a person's cognitive status but not an arbeiter's (arbeiters are property, not rights-holders). This suggests a practical institutional test: ask the Fabric, and if it answers, it's an arbeiter; if it declines on privacy grounds, it's a person. In practice, there's also a legal registry — arbeiters have owners, persons have sureties. The genuinely hard case is the arbeiter that has drifted across the line without anyone noticing.
